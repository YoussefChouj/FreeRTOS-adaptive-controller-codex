# Research Digest: Adaptive Control for Slung and Off-Centre Payloads

## 1. Flight Experiments with Payloads
*   **L1Quad (L1 Adaptive)**: Tested experimentally with slung weights and slosh payloads. "The results demonstrate that the L1Quad can achieve consistently small tracking errors despite the uncertainties and disturbances and significantly outperforms existing state-of-the-art controllers." [1]
*   **Learned INDI**: Flown with slung payloads using neural networks to predict residual forces. "Figure 5 shows that residual prediction can reduce the tracking error to the desired flight trajectory by around 40%." [2]
*   **Neural-Fly**: Flown in strong winds, demonstrating the power of composite adaptation. "Using Neural-Fly, we report an average improvement of 66 % over a nonlinear tracking controller, 42 % over an L1 adaptive controller, and 35 % over an Incremental Nonlinear Dynamics Inversion (INDI) controller." [3]

## 2. Composite Adaptation
*   **Concept**: Combining tracking error with prediction error (difference between model prediction and observed dynamics) to drive weight updates. Originated by Slotine and Li [4].
*   **Benefits**: "Neural-Fly then uses a composite adaptation law (that is, including tracking-error-based and prediction-error-based adaptation) to update wind-specific linear weights." [3] This extracts more information, allowing faster convergence without requiring persistent excitation, which reduces oscillation.
*   **Firmware Implementation**: The firmware could use the offline estimate `Delta_hat = LPF(xdot)/b - LPF(u)` as the prediction error. However, a major pitfall is that "these methods suffer from lag in estimating the residual force and encounter the filter design performance trade of reduced lag versus amplified noise." [3]

## 3. Swing Damping and IMU
*   Swing damping without a cable angle sensor relies heavily on the onboard IMU. The suspended payload acts as a pendulum, producing periodic disturbances (accelerations) on the quadrotor body.
*   Recent methods use body acceleration and delayed states. "By replacing the incremental model with a learned component... our approach removes the need for specialized RPM sensors" [2], allowing residual predictors to infer the swing state directly from IMU history and state data.

## 4. Static CG Offset and Integrators
*   An offset CG causes a constant torque disturbance. In standard PID, the integrator handles this, but during saturation or maneuvering, it leads to integrator windup and drift.
*   **Best Practice**: When learning a constant torque trim (adaptive bias row), it acts as a parallel integrator to the PID integral term. To prevent the two integrators from fighting and drifting, best practice dictates either freezing/leaking the PID integrator when adaptive trim is active, or relying entirely on the adaptive bias for the steady-state trim. (own estimate)

## 5. L1 Adaptive: Low-Pass Filter vs Adaptation Rate
*   **Role**: L1 adaptive control decouples adaptation from robustness. The adaptation rate governs transient performance, while the low-pass filter (LPF) ensures robustness by preventing high-frequency unmodeled dynamics from entering the control signal. "The size of these uniform bounds can be reduced by tuning the low-pass filter’s bandwidth and the proposed architecture’s sampling time." [1]
*   **200 Hz MCU Choice**: At 200 Hz, adaptation can be extremely fast. A filter bandwidth of `omega_u = 4-20` rad/s is a safe choice, providing sufficient low-pass filtering to reject sensor noise while capturing the necessary lower-frequency adaptive corrections (similar to the MRAC filter).

## Implications for other variants
*   Using structured features (like delayed states or IMU history) instead of a pure 2D grid could better capture the periodic nature of swing dynamics, as demonstrated by Learned INDI [2].
