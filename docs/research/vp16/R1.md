## 1. Gaussian RBF centre and width selection

### Sanner & Slotine spacing-vs-width rule

Sanner & Slotine (IEEE TNN 1992) provide the foundational framework for Gaussian RBF networks
in direct adaptive control. Their approach arranges Gaussian units on a **regular lattice** over
a compact operating domain and derives the required density from the assumed smoothness of
the unknown function.

> "direct adaptive tracking control of a class of continuous-time nonlinear
> dynamic systems" — Sanner & Slotine, IEEE TNN 3(6):837–863, 1992 [S1]

The key design rule: the width σ and spacing Δ of the lattice must satisfy a relationship such
that neighbouring Gaussians **overlap sufficiently** to guarantee a desired approximation error ε
over the compact set. For a regular lattice the standard choice is σ ≈ Δ (the inter-centre
distance), ensuring each point in the domain is covered by at least one substantially active basis.

### Non-uniform centres: "width = gap to nearest neighbour"

Setting σ_i = dist(c_i, c_nearest) is a widely used heuristic for non-uniform grids.

> "The width is set to the Euclidean distance between a center and its
> closest neighboring center" — common heuristic documented in MATLAB RBF
> toolbox documentation [S2]

**Assessment for vp16:** This heuristic is sound as a starting point but has two risks:
- **Holes:** Where centres are sparse, σ equals a large gap. The Gaussian drops below useful
  activation in the interior of the gap if the gap is much larger than σ. A safer rule is
  σ_i = α · dist(c_i, c_nearest) with α ≈ 1.0–1.5, i.e. modest overlap factor.
- **Over-smoothing near dense clusters:** Where centres are closely packed (e.g. near hover at
  ±0.05 normalised), a small σ makes each bump very narrow. With per-weight limits at 1% of
  authority this is acceptable, but γ must be high enough to fill those narrow bumps.

### Normalised RBF (partition of unity) vs raw Gaussians + bias

Normalised RBFs divide each Gaussian by the sum of all Gaussians, enforcing Σ ψ̂_i = 1.

> "the output effectively acts as a locally weighted average of the weights"
> — property of normalised RBF partition of unity [S3]

**Outside the grid (extrapolation):**
- Raw Gaussians decay to zero. With a bias row (φ_0 = 1), the output far from any centre
  equals the bias weight: a learned DC offset. This is the vp16 design.
- Normalised RBFs approach the weighted average of the outermost weights, which may be
  nonsensical. They do not decay to zero, but they also do not produce a meaningful estimate.

**Effect on ||Φ||² and the γ rule:**
- Raw Gaussians: ||Φ||² is small far from centres (only the bias row contributes ≈ 1), so
  γ_eff = γ/(1+||Φ||²) ≈ γ/2. Near hover where many bumps activate, ||Φ||² can reach
  O(N_active). The normalised-gradient denominator (1+||Φ||²) naturally limits γ.
- Normalised RBFs: ||Φ||² ≤ 1 everywhere (since Σ ψ̂_i = 1 and each ψ̂_i ≤ 1).
  This makes ||Φ||² uninformative for the normalised-gradient scaling — γ_eff ≈ γ/2 everywhere.

**Verdict for vp16:** Raw Gaussians + bias row is the correct choice. The normalised-gradient
law already handles ||Φ||² scaling. Normalisation would defeat the purpose of the bias row
and make extrapolation behaviour less predictable.

## 2. Tensor-product 2-D grids vs other bases

| Basis | Locality | Curse of dim. | Stability analysis | UAV adaptive control use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **RBF (Gaussian)** | Local | Grid grows as O(N^d) | Straightforward (linear-in-params) | Most common [S4] |
| **CMAC / B-spline** | Local, sparse | Better (local support, fewer weights active) | Good (piecewise) | Used in SHL flight ctrl [S5] |
| **Single-hidden-layer NN** | Global | No grid | Harder (nonlinear weights) | Calise & Rysdyk 1998 [S6] |
| **Chebyshev polynomials** | Global | Low for smooth f | Moderate | Trajectory opt, not adaptive [S7] |

> "RBF networks are the most common choice due to their proven performance
> in compensating for unpredictable, nonlinear disturbances" — survey of
> basis functions in UAV adaptive control [S4]

> "B-splines provide high-quality, smooth approximations with a sparse
> structure, which is computationally efficient" — B-spline basis comparison [S5]

**For vp16's 2-D tensor grids (8×4 = 32 own + 4×4 = 16 cross = 48 per axis):** The RBF
tensor-product grid is well-suited. At 48 weights/axis × 6 axes = 288 total weights, the
curse of dimensionality is not a concern. B-splines would save some computation (only
≈4 active per query vs ≈4–8 for Gaussians), but the stability analysis for Gaussian
RBF + normalised gradient + projection is better established.

## 3. Learning rate (gamma) for normalised-gradient MRAC

### Discrete-time stability bound at 200 Hz

Lavretsky & Wise (Robust and Adaptive Control, Springer 2013) derive the discrete-time
stability condition for MRAC. The adaptation gain Γ must satisfy a bound inversely
proportional to the sampling period T.

> "Excessive learning rates in discrete-time adaptive control can lead to
> oscillations or instability" — discrete-time MRAC stability [S8]

The general rule: for a normalised-gradient law, the effective gain per step is
γ_eff · T · ||Φ||² / (1+||Φ||²). Stability requires this to remain below ≈ 2 (from the
Lyapunov decrease condition). At T = 5 ms (200 Hz), with ||Φ||² ≈ 4–8 (a few active
Gaussians), γ_eff < 2/(T · ||Φ||²/(1+||Φ||²)) ≈ 2/(0.005 · 0.8) = 500.

### Fill-time rule (vp16 design)

The vp16 γ rule: γ = lim · (1+||Φ||²) / (e_sat · T_fill). With T_fill = 3 s, e_sat as the
saturation threshold, and lim as the per-weight limit. This ensures a single weight reaches
its limit in T_fill seconds under sustained maximum error. At 200 Hz this gives
γ = lim/(e_sat · 3) · (1+||Φ||²), which is typically 10–100 depending on axis.

### Why high gamma causes oscillation

High γ → fast weight changes → effective controller gain changes faster than the plant
bandwidth → oscillations at frequency ≈ plant bandwidth × √(γ·T). The normalised-gradient
denominator (1+||Φ||²) partially mitigates this, but projection limits are the primary
safeguard.

## 4. Robust modifications: σ-mod vs e-mod vs projection vs dead-zone

### Comparison

Lavretsky & Gibson (arXiv:1112.4232, 2011) provide the definitive treatment of the
projection operator:

> "The projection algorithm is frequently used in adaptive control and this
> note presents a detailed analysis of its properties" — Lavretsky & Gibson [S9]

The paper proves (Lemma 9): if θ(0) ∈ Ω₁ and f(θ) is convex, then θ(t) ∈ Ω₁ ∀t ≥ 0.
Per-weight bounds are enforced through per-component convex functions f_i.

| Method | Drift prevention | Unlearning? | Bias error | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **σ-modification** | Pulls θ → 0 | Yes: θ decays with time const 1/σ | Steady-state bias O(σ) | Simple, ok when true θ ≈ 0 |
| **e-modification** | Active only when e large | Less than σ-mod | Smaller bias | Better for nonzero θ* |
| **Projection** | Hard wall per weight | No: weights stay at learned value | Zero (within bounds) | Best for known bounds [S10] |
| **Dead-zone** | Stops learning when |e| < δ | No | None within δ | Noise-dominated regime |

> "The modification adds a term... that forces the parameter estimate toward
> the origin in the absence of a strong tracking error" — σ-modification
> decay property, Ioannou & Sun 1996 [S11]

### For low excitation in hover

Hover = low excitation = parameters not refreshed. **σ-modification** with σ = 0.01 causes
the learned trim to decay with τ = 1/0.01 = 100 s. A bias weight holding 15% of authority
decays to 37% of its value in 100 s — this IS a problem for a static trim.

**Recommendation for vp16:**
- **Projection** on every weight (already in the design: per-weight limits of 1% authority for
  bumps, 15% for bias). This prevents drift without any unlearning.
- **σ-modification at σ = 0.01** on the non-bias Gaussian weights only. The bias row should
  NOT have σ-modification, or should use a much smaller σ (e.g. 0.001), so its learned trim
  decays with τ = 1000 s, acceptable for a 5-min flight.
- e-modification is a viable alternative: it only activates when the error is large, so in
  calm hover the learned weights are preserved.

### Bursting

Bursting occurs when parameters drift far from true values during low excitation, then the
system encounters excitation and the large parameter error causes a transient spike.
Projection eliminates this entirely (parameters cannot drift beyond bounds). σ-modification
reduces it but introduces the unlearning trade-off.

## 5. Concurrent learning (Chowdhary & Johnson)

### Theory

Chowdhary & Johnson (JGCD 34(2):592–607, 2011) introduced concurrent learning (CL): the
adaptive law uses both instantaneous tracking error AND a stored buffer of past data points
to update weights.

> "enables parameter convergence without the need for strict persistent
> excitation" — concurrent learning key property [S12]

The key requirement: the stored data must satisfy a **rank condition** (the regression matrix
formed from stored Φ vectors must have full column rank). When this is met, CL guarantees
**exponential convergence** of both tracking and parameter errors.

### Flight results with RBF on rotorcraft

Chowdhary et al. validated CL on quadrotors in MIT's RAVEN environment (2013). Key result:
a baseline controller was rapidly **transferred** between quadrotors with different inertias,
using ≈50 stored data points to satisfy the rank condition.

> "rapidly improve the trajectory-tracking performance of a quadrotor...
> eliminates the need for time-intensive manual retuning" — CL quadrotor
> flight tests [S13]

### Would CL fix low persistence of excitation near hover?

**Yes, in principle.** CL's whole purpose is to work without PE. If the data buffer was
populated during earlier manoeuvres (takeoff, initial hover oscillations), it provides the
"memory" of informative states that hover alone cannot generate.

### CPU and memory cost

For N basis functions and a buffer of p_max points:
- **Memory:** p_max × (dim_x + N) floats. With N = 48 per axis, p_max = 50, dim_x ≈ 4:
  50 × 52 × 4 bytes ≈ 10 kB per axis, ≈ 60 kB total. Feasible on STM32F4 (192 kB SRAM).
- **CPU:** Each adaptation step sums over p_max stored points: p_max × N multiplies per axis.
  50 × 48 = 2400 MACs per axis × 6 axes = 14.4k MACs per step. At 200 Hz on Cortex-M4F
  (≈1 MAC/cycle at 168 MHz), this is ≈ 14.4k/168M ≈ 86 μs — feasible but a meaningful
  fraction of the 5 ms budget. The singular-value-maximizing data selection adds O(N²) per
  new candidate point, ≈ 2304 operations — negligible.

**Bottom line:** CL is implementable on STM32F4 for vp16's basis size, but adds ~86 μs/step
and ~60 kB RAM. This is a strong candidate for a vp17 upgrade after vp16 demonstrates basic
stability.

## 6. Phase lag of tracking-error-driven RBF adaptation

### The fundamental problem

A tracking-error-driven adaptation law reacts AFTER the error appears. For a periodic
disturbance at frequency ω, the adapted compensation has an inherent phase lag determined
by the effective adaptation bandwidth ω_ad ≈ γ·||Φ||²/(1+||Φ||²). Our measurements show
u_ad phase of -70 to -101 deg at 0.25–0.9 Hz.

For a 0.4 Hz disturbance: ω = 2π·0.4 ≈ 2.5 rad/s. The u_ad LPF at ω_u = 4 rad/s adds
≈ -32 deg at 2.5 rad/s. The adaptation loop adds further delay: with effective ω_ad ≈ 10
rad/s, the adaptation adds ≈ -14 deg. Total ≈ -46 deg, growing worse as adaptation slows
(lower γ or ||Φ||²).

### Can a static map Φ(drone state) represent a disturbance from a hidden load state?

**No, not directly.** The pendulum load torque depends on load swing angle and velocity, which
are hidden states not measured by the drone's IMU. A static map from drone state (rate, angle)
to torque cannot capture the phase relationship of the pendulum — the same drone angle can
correspond to load-swinging-left or load-swinging-right, producing opposite torques.

> "observers such as Extended State Observers (ESO) or High-Gain Disturbance
> Observers are frequently integrated to estimate external disturbances" —
> quadrotor slung load control [S14]

### Which inputs fix it?

1. **Delayed states / time-delay embedding:** Including x(t-τ), x(t-2τ) as additional basis
   function inputs breaks the ambiguity. Takens' embedding theorem guarantees that for a
   sufficiently high embedding dimension, the hidden state can be reconstructed. Cost: doubles
   or triples the input dimension → RBF grid size explodes.

2. **Accelerometer signals:** The drone's accelerometer directly senses the load force. Adding
   a_z (or filtered a_z) as an input to the basis functions lets the RBF distinguish
   "load pulling left" from "load pulling right". This is the cheapest solution for vp16.

3. **Disturbance observer (ESO/HESO):** A harmonic extended state observer tuned to the swing
   frequency (0.4 Hz) can estimate the load torque with much less phase lag. The estimated
   disturbance is fed forward as cancellation. This is the most principled solution but
   adds observer design complexity.

4. **L1 adaptive control architecture:** Decouples adaptation rate from control bandwidth
   using a low-pass filter. Allows very high γ (fast adaptation) while the filter prevents
   high-frequency instability. This directly addresses the phase-lag problem.

| Raw Gaussians + bias row is correct (not normalised RBF) | Partition-of-unity analysis [S3] | No change needed; bias row handles DC offset correctly | high |

| Static Φ(drone state) cannot track hidden load state | Slung load control literature [S14] | Add accelerometer input to basis, or add observer | high |

## Implications for other variants

- **S6/S10 (structured features):** The 6 structured features (rate, tilt products) are a form
  of hand-crafted basis. They cannot represent hidden load states either. Adding accel or
  delayed-state features would help all variants.
- **RBF24T (24-Gaussian grid on rate×tilt):** The 24T grid uses a single 2-D tensor product.
  The analysis above applies directly. Width selection matters most for the sparse outer centres.
- **L1 filter variant:** Raising the output filter cutoff (ω_u) trades noise rejection for
  phase lag. The L1 architecture formally decouples γ from ω_u, allowing both to be set
  independently. This is the cleanest path to reducing phase lag.
